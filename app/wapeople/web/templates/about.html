About us

We built WA People so that everyone in Washington can easily see the laws being made for them, and who is making them.

Thousands of bills are introduced in Olympia every two years. They touch rent and housing, schools, taxes, health care, public safety, jobs, the environment, and nearly every part of daily life. The information is public, but it's scattered across many pages and written in legislative shorthand. It's hard to follow unless following it is your job.

WA People puts it in one place and in plain language:

  • Every bill, proposed, ongoing, and past, back to 1991, with a short summary of what it does.
  • Who brought it: the prime sponsor and every co-sponsor.
  • How every legislator voted, on every recorded vote, with dates.
  • Who represents you, on a map of all 49 legislative districts.
  • What the courts said: Washington Supreme Court decisions about laws and public policy.
  • Who funds them: money trails for every legislator, statewide official and Supreme Court justice: how much they raised, who gave it, where it came from, and where it went.

We believe an informed public makes for better government. WA People is nonpartisan: it doesn't endorse candidates, parties, or positions, and it reports the official record as it is. It's free, has no ads and no tracking, and is built entirely with open-source software and public data. It updates itself every day.

See something wrong, or have an idea? Contact us. Corrections help everyone.

How it works

The rest of this page explains where the data comes from and how we organize it.

Where the data comes from

  • Bills, sponsors, history and votes: the Washington State Legislature's public web services, the same data behind app.leg.wa.gov.
  • Supreme Court decisions: CourtListener, the free legal archive run by the nonprofit Free Law Project. Full opinion text comes from the official PDFs published by the Washington Courts.
  • Campaign money and financial statements: the Washington Public Disclosure Commission's open data on data.wa.gov.

This is not an official government website. For anything that matters legally, check the official bill page or the opinion itself. Every page here links to its official source.

How bills are sorted

The Legislature reports a short status code for each bill, such as H Approps, S Rules X or C 209 L 25. WA People turns it into plain language and puts every bill into one of three groups:

  • Proposed: prefiled, introduced, or sitting in a policy or fiscal committee.
  • Ongoing: out of committee, on a floor calendar, passed one chamber, or passed by the Legislature and waiting for the Governor.
  • Past: enacted (with its chapter in the session laws), vetoed, adopted, confirmed, or did not pass.

Whether a bill “passed the House” or “passed the Senate” comes from its recorded final-passage roll call. Washington's constitution (Article II, section 22) requires a recorded yea/nay vote on final passage. A bill that hasn't passed when the second year of a two-year session ends is listed as did not pass, together with how far it got (for example, “passed House, stalled in Senate”). Bill numbers restart every two years, so HB 1217 in 2025–26 and HB 1217 in 2023–24 are different bills.

Known gaps in the source data: the Legislature's services have no step-by-step bill history before 2001, though sponsors, final status and roll-call votes go back to 1991. A few later bills have incomplete history, and a handful of roll calls are missing or labeled inconsistently (for example “FP” for final passage, or a blank motion). A bill that became law or was vetoed is always counted as having passed both chambers, even when one chamber's roll call is missing.

Bill summaries

The short summary under each bill comes from the Legislature's own nonpartisan staff: the Bill Analyses and Bill Reports written for committee hearings and floor votes (or, for older sessions, bill digests). WA People uses the report on the version of the bill that advanced furthest, so an enacted bill's summary describes what became law. Each bill page links to the full report. Bills that never got a hearing have no staff report; for those the Legislature's one-line description is shown instead. Summaries are not written or edited by this site.

Attorney and judicial discipline cases are left out. When an opinion cites a session law (“Laws of 2021, ch. 196”), it is linked to the exact bill that became that chapter. When it names a bill with a year next to it (“ESSB 5096 … 2021”), it is linked by number and session. References without a year are listed but not linked, because the same bill number is reused every session.

How the money trails work

Candidates in Washington must report the money they raise and spend to the Public Disclosure Commission (PDC). WA People loads those reports for each person who holds a state legislative seat, a statewide office, or a seat on the Supreme Court, plus former legislators. It covers every Washington campaign in the PDC's records for that person, not only the race for their current office. Each record links to the report it came from.

  • Who is included: legislators come from the Legislature's roster. Statewide officials and justices are on a list checked against the Court Directory and PDC election results. Federal officeholders report to the Federal Election Commission instead and aren't included, nor are county and city officials.
  • How people are matched: a legislator is linked to PDC records only when the last name, the first name and a campaign for the same seat at the right time all agree. When they don't, we show no records instead of guessing. A few are linked by hand, with a note on the page.
  • How far back it goes: the PDC's electronic candidate records begin with the 2009–10 election cycle, so earlier careers don't appear. Campaigns that use the PDC's "mini reporting" option file no itemized records, only a total.
  • How donors are grouped: "where it came from" uses the category each filer reported. Organization names are grouped when they differ only by capitalization, punctuation, or a fixed list of abbreviations (WA, PAC, Assn., and shorthand for the main party and caucus committees), so one organization can still appear under several spellings. Small gifts that campaigns may report as a lump sum have no name or location. "Miscellaneous receipts" aren't gifts: they're often money moved from the candidate's earlier campaign or surplus account, which then counts again in the campaign that receives it. An individual's employer shows where they work; the employer didn't give that money.
  • Spending categories: newer reports include a category. For older ones, a small set of plain keyword rules reads the filer's description, and anything those rules don't clearly recognize is shown as "not categorized."
  • The next step back: when a donor's name matches a registered political committee by those same rules, its page shows who funds that committee. Names that don't match aren't guessed at, so this trail can stop short.
  • Checking the totals: every day each campaign's count and total of records are compared with the PDC's own, and any campaign that differs is reloaded. Reports can be amended, so figures can change after a filing.
  • What we leave out on purpose: street addresses, phone numbers and emails, which the source publishes, aren't copied here. Personal financial statements omit home addresses, family members' names, and anything reported only for a dependent. Individual donors' listings are kept out of search engines. The official filings at the PDC have the complete record.

A contribution isn't an accusation. These records show who gave money, not that anyone received anything in return. WA People doesn't connect donations to votes or draw conclusions from them.

Condition of release, from the PDC: this data constitutes a list of individuals and may not be used for commercial purposes (RCW 42.56.070(9) and AGO 1975 No. 15). WA People is free and has no ads or sponsors.

Open-source stack
